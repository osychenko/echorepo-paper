Stakeholder-informed design and operational evaluation of ECHOREPO}

\begin{abstract}
Large-scale soil citizen science requires more than an application for submitting observations: physical samples are subsequently handled by laboratories, additional measurements are produced asynchronously, data-quality questions emerge during operation, and different users require different forms of access. We present \repo, a stakeholder-informed, open-source research-data infrastructure developed within the European ECHO project, which targets approximately 16,000 soil samples and intends laboratory analysis for nearly all successfully collected specimens, subject to technical and logistical losses. ECHOREPO uses established software components to support five interacting requirements: adaptation to stakeholder needs, persistent application-independent sample identity, incremental integration of heterogeneous results, contextual quality assessment and recoverable correction procedures, and controlled versioned publication. We describe these design choices, the implementation of web and programmatic interfaces, and the available operational evidence. In the 29 October 2026 canonical dataset (version 12), 10,554 samples were represented, including 8,744 with photographs, 6,443 with published laboratory measurements and 1,200 with biodiversity summaries. These figures describe a dated stage of an ongoing laboratory programme, not final analytical completeness. Source-side screening identified 95 default-coordinate cases and 301 planned-country discrepancies, but verified error and recovery rates have not been measured. Six linked CSV resources, CSV on the Web metadata and versioned Zenodo publication provide a citable research product. The methodological contribution is an integrated and operationally demonstrated data-lifecycle approach, together with lessons and evaluation boundaries; workshop-to-implementation traceability, longitudinal enrichment and recovery effectiveness require further systematic evidence.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} citizen science; stakeholder-informed design; environmental informatics; research-data lifecycle; asynchronous enrichment; provenance; quality assurance; FAIR data.

\section{Introduction}
Citizen-science projects involve participants with different skills, motivations and research expectations. Their information systems must simultaneously support accessible public participation and the needs of scientists who require traceable, interpretable and reusable data \parencite{bonney2009citizenscience,bonney2014nextsteps}. This dual role is especially demanding in soil monitoring: field observations are associated with physical samples that must subsequently be transported, analysed and enriched with laboratory measurements, sometimes weeks or months after collection \parencite{peiro2026soil}. A sample therefore follows a distributed research-data lifecycle rather than a single digital submission event.

Two kinds of evolution must be accommodated. First, the \emph{scientific record} changes as photographs, elemental measurements and microbial biodiversity results become available from separate organisations and schedules. Second, the \emph{information system} changes as citizen scientists, ambassadors, data curators and researchers encounter new requirements and operational problems. Such changes may affect the user interface, search and discovery tools, quality-control procedures, access policies and programmatic data services. Reproducible publication must nevertheless preserve a stable research identity and make successive data versions interpretable independently of the collection application.

Neither established technological components nor participatory development are, by themselves, novel scientific methods. Previous citizen-science infrastructures have addressed curation, validation and sharing \parencite{wang2015citsci,sullivan2014ebird}; FAIR, CSVW and provenance-related specifications provide relevant standards \parencite{wilkinson2016fair,w3c2015csvw,w3c2013provo,soiland2022rocrate}. In information-systems research, design science examines whether constructed artefacts address a documented problem and what transferable design knowledge can be obtained from their evaluation \parencite{hevner2004design,peffers2007dsrm}. Here the interest is not a new database engine or a claim of first implementation, but the integration of stakeholder-informed design, asynchronous sample-centred enrichment, provenance-aware quality handling and independent scientific publication in one operating infrastructure.

We investigate four questions: (RQ1) How can the infrastructure accommodate the distinct and evolving access and information needs of participants, ambassadors, curators and scientific users? (RQ2) How can records created at different stages retain a stable application-independent identity as new analyses arrive? (RQ3) How can contextual checks support investigation and correction without treating every inconsistency as a verified error or discarding valid evidence? (RQ4) How can evolving operational data be released as dated, independently interpretable research products? These questions define five design requirements: stakeholder adaptation, persistent identity, incremental enrichment, contextual QA and versioned publication.

We examine these requirements through \repo, developed for the European ECHO soil citizen-science project \parencite{echo2023project}. The campaign aims to collect approximately 16,000 samples, with laboratory analysis intended for nearly all successfully collected samples except unavoidable technical or logistical losses. The fraction already associated with laboratory data at a given date reflects an ongoing processing and publication workflow, not a permanent selection fraction. Our evaluation combines implemented functionality, a dated canonical snapshot and source-side QA flag counts. We distinguish these observations from outcomes that are not yet documented in sufficient detail, notably causal attribution of design changes to individual workshops, quantitative usability improvements, error-recovery success and longitudinal laboratory turnaround.

\section{Materials and methods}
\subsection{Study context and architecture}
ECHOREPO was developed within ECHO (Engaging Citizens in Soil Science: The Road to Healthier Soils; Horizon Europe Grant No.~101112869, with UKRI support under Grant No.~10068004). Participants enter observations, coordinates and photographs in the EchoSoil mobile application, which uses an independent Firebase-based backend and authentication system. Dedicated ingestion routines retrieve source records, reconcile identifiers and transform the incoming representation into a canonical research model. The repository is therefore not a direct extension of the mobile application's database.

The architecture separates acquisition, ingestion and harmonisation, canonical storage, and controlled publication (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The implementation uses Flask for web/API access, PostgreSQL for structured research data, MinIO-compatible object storage for images and other files, Keycloak/OpenID Connect for authenticated use, and Docker Compose for deployment. These are concrete implementation choices, not prerequisites of the architectural pattern. The defining boundary is between source-specific adapters and the canonical research-data representation.

\subsection{Stakeholder-informed requirements and iterative development}
The ECHO project combines research activities with citizen and ambassador participation, including co-creation activities in its broader engagement programme \parencite{echo2024ambassadors}. During the development of ECHOREPO, project collaborators report using co-design sessions and feedback from citizen scientists, ambassadors and scientific users to inform both backend functionality and access interfaces. For the present study, this is treated as a \emph{reported development process}, rather than a formally evaluated participatory-design experiment. Published project-wide co-creation activities do not, by themselves, establish that a specific ECHOREPO feature resulted from a particular workshop.

We distinguish the stakeholder groups by their intended interaction with the research-data lifecycle. Citizen scientists and ambassadors require comprehensible exploration of submitted samples and project results; scientific users require search, analytical metadata, structured downloads and repeatable queries; curators require mechanisms to identify inconsistent source information and investigate corrections; maintainers require controlled access, auditability and publication workflows. These categories provide an analytic framework for describing design choices, without implying that every participant expressed each requirement or that a formal requirements survey was conducted.

Implementation examples are examined through currently available software functionality: the web-based map and sample-search interfaces, country and biogeographical-region filters, localised presentation, sample-detail access, authenticated API endpoints and canonical exports. We separate \emph{verification that a feature exists} from \emph{verification of its origin or impact}. A full traceability assessment would link dated workshop minutes, user feedback or issue reports to a requirement, a design decision, a code change and subsequent evaluation. Such a workshop-to-change matrix and systematic usability outcomes are not available in the present manuscript, so individual feature changes are not assigned causal effects or participant counts here.

This distinction is important for the design-science framing: iterative stakeholder input motivates and constrains an artefact, but a methodological finding about participation requires a transparent account of how input was collected, interpreted and validated. The case study therefore uses operational functionality as implementation evidence, and identifies stakeholder-to-decision tracing as an explicit outstanding evaluation task \parencite{hevner2004design,peffers2007dsrm}.

\subsection{Operational lifecycle and evaluation requirements}
The ECHO campaign and its laboratory programme are ongoing. A physical sample may be collected, registered, transported, analysed, returned as one or more source files, ingested into ECHOREPO, canonicalised and included in a dated publication. These phases need not occur in a fixed order for every analytical domain, and an additional result can arrive after an earlier dataset release. The stages describe the scientific workflow, not a claim that every stage is currently represented by a complete, machine-readable per-sample status or event log.

This distinction determines how incompleteness must be interpreted. Absence of a laboratory row in a canonical export may mean that analysis is pending, the result has not yet been received or ingested, the sample was lost, or the result was omitted under a publication filter. The canonical exports alone cannot reliably distinguish these cases; that would require reconciliation with dispatch, laboratory and ingestion records. We therefore distinguish \emph{observed publication coverage at time $t$} from eventual analytical completion.

The five design requirements and their presently available evidence are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:requirements}. They are operational design criteria, not a claim that ECHOREPO is the first system to satisfy them.

\subsection{Evaluation metrics and data scope}
Our empirical unit is a distinct non-empty \code{sample\_id} represented in \code{samples.csv} in the ECHOREPO canonical version 12 snapshot of 29 October 2026 \parencite{echorepo2026dataset}. Let $S_t$ be the set of published sample identifiers at snapshot time $t$, and let $D_{d,t}$ be the set of identifiers appearing in a supplementary domain $d$ (photographs, laboratory parameters or biodiversity summaries). Snapshot coverage is $|S_t\cap D_{d,t}|/|S_t|$. Each sample counts once per domain regardless of the number of rows associated with it. We also count the number of distinct supplementary domains linked to each sample, from zero to three.

These are measurements of \emph{canonical publication coverage at a specified time}, not the proportion of samples selected for analysis, laboratory throughput, final campaign completeness or the success rate of sample transport. The campaign target of approximately 16,000 is contextual and is not used as a denominator for the domain-coverage statistics. Laboratory work is ongoing, and most successfully collected samples are planned to receive laboratory analysis subject to technical and logistical losses. The laboratory canonicalisation excludes oxide-form parameter rows and values below its default export threshold of 0.01; biodiversity coverage represents exported taxonomic summaries rather than raw marker-by-feature matrices. The effect of these exclusions should be assessed separately before interpreting absence or low values scientifically.

Quantifying laboratory lag would require at least sample-collection date, laboratory receipt or analysis date, result ingestion date and publication date, preferably with sample-level stage outcomes. The present single-snapshot analysis does not estimate these intervals. The quality-assurance tally is derived from a separate source-side report. Its 396 flagged records have not yet been reconciled identifier by identifier with $S_t$, so a population-wide QA percentage is not calculated. The planned-country check additionally requires an explicit eligible denominator of samples with usable recorded-country and kit-allocation information.

\subsection{Quality assurance and provenance-assisted assessment}
The quality-assurance (QA) workflow flags inconsistent source records while retaining their sample identifiers and provenance. The present assessment distinguishes two classes. First, \emph{application-default coordinates} are placeholder positions recorded when the mobile workflow does not obtain a usable location; they cannot be treated as measured sampling positions. Second, a \emph{planned-country mismatch} occurs when the country assigned to a QR code for sample distribution differs from the country associated with its actual recorded collection location. This comparison is independent of the jittered public map coordinates. A planned-country mismatch can arise from a legitimate cross-country redistribution of sampling kits, outdated allocation metadata or an inaccurate location; it does not alone establish an erroneous GPS record.

Operational handling distinguishes at least four decisions: detection of a potential problem, examination of available provenance, correction or qualification supported by evidence, and retention of the scientific record and its identity. Project procedures are intended to enable recovery of usable records rather than systematic removal after a flag. However, the present evidence establishes the existence of screening classes, not the proportion of cases resolved, the number successfully recovered, or completeness of a per-record correction history. Assessing those outcomes would require a case-level decision log and independent audit.

Flagged records can be investigated using the QR/sample identifier, kit-allocation history, source observations and other appropriately protected provenance. A correction should be made only when supported by evidence; otherwise the flag remains unresolved or qualified rather than being silently overwritten. Thus, detection, interpretation and verified recovery are distinct stages. The classification cannot by itself identify causes such as device malfunction, participant error or workflow interruption, and recovery outcomes were not measured in this evaluation \parencite{kosmala2016quality}.

\subsection{Geographical privacy and image protection}
Exact source coordinates are retained in the controlled-access environment and assessed for QA before deriving public positions. Public sample coordinates are deterministically displaced within a configurable maximum distance, currently 1,000~m by default. This is a pragmatic compromise intended to reduce precision around individual properties while maintaining regional-scale utility; it is \emph{not} a formally validated anonymity threshold. The field \code{coordinate\_obfuscation\_radius\_m} documents the configured radius in the published sample table.

Displacement is not constrained to coastlines or administrative boundaries. Consequently, a valid sample close to a border may appear in a neighbouring country, or a coastal sample may appear at sea. These visual artefacts do not indicate that the source coordinates failed QA: country-mismatch checks operate on underlying coordinates. ECHOREPO also supports sample exploration by EEA 2016 biogeographical regions \parencite{eea2016biogeo}; membership is precomputed from underlying coordinates rather than inferred from the public marker position. Analysts should not use displaced positions for parcel-level, boundary-sensitive or fine-resolution spatial inference.



Photographs require complementary protection. The MinIO image-redaction workflow locally detects text regions (potentially including street signs, building numbers and registration plates), frontal faces and QR codes, then strongly blurs or masks detected areas. Image re-encoding removes embedded EXIF fields, including GPS metadata. Object-version markers and detection counts support resumable processing and auditing. Detection is imperfect, especially for oblique or small text, non-frontal faces and low-quality images. Moreover, the standalone redactor operates \emph{after} image mirroring; without private staging, it does not guarantee that an unredacted object was never briefly accessible. These are limitations of the current implementation, not guarantees of anonymity.

\subsection{Canonical publication and Zenodo synchronisation}
Operational tables evolve as new observations and analytical results are ingested. Stable publication products are instead generated through canonical export interfaces. The Zenodo synchronisation routine retrieves an authenticated export bundle from ECHOREPO, extracts and validates the six canonical CSV resources, checks declared schemas and selected reference integrity, and generates a \code{file.json} metadata document following CSV on the Web (CSVW) \parencite{w3c2015csvw}. The metadata describes columns, types, identifiers and primary/foreign-key relationships; it also records dataset-level information and selected semantic mappings. The original ZIP may optionally be retained alongside the individual resources.

For updates, the publication routine creates a new version draft of an existing Zenodo deposition, replaces the files as configured, uploads the validated resources and CSVW document, verifies the remote filenames, and publishes the release. It records the deposition, version and concept identifiers together with publication status and source-file information in a local log. The result is an independently citable snapshot, distinct from the mutable operational database \parencite{zenodo2026versioning,echorepo2026dataset}.

The published metadata and persistent identifiers are designed to support indexing and discovery through infrastructures such as OpenAIRE and SoilWise \parencite{soilwise2026faq}. Nevertheless, a Zenodo deposit and CSVW document are evidence of implemented FAIR-oriented mechanisms, not proof of complete FAIR compliance or independently verified discovery in every external catalogue.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Implemented interfaces and stakeholder-facing services}
The deployed software exposes complementary interfaces for distinct tasks. The interactive web application supports sample exploration, detailed records and geographic filtering, including country and biogeographical-region queries. The Data API supplies authenticated machine-facing queries and canonical data exports, including formats suitable for downstream analysis. These are observable implementation results: a user can examine records through the web interface, while a researcher or processing script can retrieve structured information without depending on the collection application's internal Firebase representation.

The software also contains quality-screening utilities and versioned publication tooling. Together, these facilities show how the same sample-centred data model can serve exploratory, curation and computational workflows. They are not evidence, by themselves, that any specific interface feature improved usability, participation, error rates or research productivity. The development team reports that stakeholder feedback informed revisions of the backend and interfaces, but a dated and auditable mapping from feedback events to particular changes has not yet been assembled for this evaluation. Accordingly, the implemented services are reported separately from unmeasured outcomes of the co-design process.

\subsection{Coverage and integration of canonical research data}
The 29 October 2026 canonical snapshot contains 10,554 unique soil samples in an ongoing campaign targeting approximately 16,000 collections. Among these, 8,744 samples (82.85\%) have associated photographic records, 6,443 (61.05\%) published laboratory measurements and 1,200 (11.37\%) microbial biodiversity results (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}). These values describe \emph{which domains were linked and published on that date}. Because laboratory analyses and result ingestion are still in progress, they cannot be interpreted as final analysis yields or as evidence that only those samples were selected for laboratory work.

The separate geographical and allocation-consistency screening report contains 396 flagged records in two reported, mutually exclusive classes: 95 application-default coordinate cases and 301 discrepancies between a QR kit's planned country and the recorded country of collection (Table~\ref{tab:qa}). The first class identifies a placeholder position rather than a measured location. The second class identifies disagreement between two sources of contextual information, not necessarily an incorrect position. A valid cross-border transfer of a sampling kit, stale assignment information or a true geolocation error could each produce a mismatch.

Such claims require case-level adjudication, flag-to-snapshot ID reconciliation, documentation of decisions and, where relevant, a denominator restricted to records eligible for each rule. \subsection{Independent publication of research resources}
The canonical sample-centred exports provide an independently interpretable representation of the operational dataset: common sample identifiers link the four sample-related tables, while parameter and method codes connect laboratory results to their definition tables. The observed domain coverage in Table~\ref{tab:coverage} demonstrates that these relations are populated across different forms of evidence; it does not imply that every sample has undergone all planned analyses.

The ECHOREPO version 12 dataset, deposited by the ECHO Consortium for the 29 October 2026 snapshot \parencite{echorepo2026dataset}, comprises six canonical CSV resources accompanied by a CSV on the Web metadata document, \code{file.json}. The export-and-publish workflow checks resource schemas and reference integrity before deposit. This provides an independently identifiable, versioned data product corresponding to the dated canonical snapshot used for the coverage analysis; the record-level QA report still requires independent population reconciliation. Appearance in any particular SoilWise catalogue also requires separate confirmation.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Scientific contribution and design-science interpretation}
The main contribution is the \emph{combined design and operational account} of a citizen-science research-data infrastructure that adapts to stakeholder needs while accumulating heterogeneous evidence about physical samples. ECHOREPO uses established technologies instead of proposing a new data-management paradigm. The methodological interest lies in how the system boundaries are defined: acquisition is decoupled from a stable sample-centred model, new analyses can be incorporated without redefining sample identity, source-aware checks support investigation rather than immediate rejection, and a controlled publication boundary produces dated research resources. Web and machine interfaces make the same underlying research data accessible for different activities.

This can be interpreted as a design-science case study \parencite{hevner2004design,peffers2007dsrm}. The implemented artefact and operating context are tangible, and the current dataset supplies evidence of populated cross-domain integration. The contribution is nevertheless not a controlled comparison of alternatives, a longitudinal usability experiment or an independently verified method for transferring the system to arbitrary projects. The stronger claim is that the study makes the requirements, chosen solutions, trade-offs and current evaluation boundaries explicit so they can inform the design of other distributed environmental research-data systems.

A practical lesson is to treat \emph{stakeholder adaptation} and \emph{scientific data stability} as complementary rather than competing priorities. Web presentation and API capabilities can evolve through feedback while canonical resource identities and versioned exports provide an interpretable contract for downstream use. Another lesson is that \emph{recoverable quality management} requires the preservation of contextual evidence: an allocation-country mismatch can be a legitimate logistics event rather than a bad GPS observation. A third lesson is to report intermediate data availability as a time-stamped stage of laboratory processing, not as a final sampling outcome. These are design principles inferred from the implementation and its documented constraints, not estimates of measured performance gains.

\subsection{What the current evaluation demonstrates}
The 10,554-sample dated snapshot demonstrates that the canonical model is populated with linked records from multiple data domains: 5,514 sample identifiers have at least two additional domains, and 997 have all three. It provides evidence that integration is implemented at operating scale. These numbers are not, on their own, measures of linkage accuracy; an independent audit should reconcile source and canonical identifiers and account for orphan records, duplicates and exclusions. Likewise, the 396 geographical and allocation flags demonstrate that context-dependent screening is performed, not that erroneous coordinates have been correctly diagnosed or recovered.

The publication boundary provides a second type of evidence. A six-resource canonical export, linked CSVW metadata, dated Zenodo version and separately archived code make the research product more readily inspectable and independently citable than the live application database. However, full computational reproducibility requires fixing the exact input snapshot, transformation code and dependencies and publishing the scripts needed to regenerate the tables and figures. Independent consumer-side reconstruction of a sample-centred research dataset from the public package would be a valuable future test, not a result already established here.

\subsection{Participatory development: potential value and evidential limits}
Citizen and ambassador participation matters here because difficulties in exploration, reporting and interpretation emerge in practice rather than only from initial software specifications. Scientific users and curators can also reveal requirements that are not apparent in the participant-facing acquisition interface, including filter semantics, data-export consistency and error provenance. ECHOREPO's distinct web, API and curation functions are consistent with these different needs. However, similarity between implemented functions and stakeholder requirements does not demonstrate that particular co-design sessions caused the implementation or that subsequent user experience improved.

For a stronger empirical evaluation, three or four documented design episodes would be more informative than an unstructured catalogue of improvements. Each should report: the stakeholder context and date; the original requirement or difficulty; the alternative design choices considered; the implemented change (supported by an issue, release or commit); and the observed outcome or remaining uncertainty. The evidence should also separate feedback from citizens and ambassadors from requirements originating with researchers or technical staff. Until those records are assembled, co-design is presented as part of the reported development method, not as a quantitatively validated intervention.

\subsection{Implications for evaluation of active citizen-science campaigns}
The distinction between \emph{not yet available} and \emph{permanently missing} should be explicit whenever research datasets are published while sample collection or laboratory analyses continue. A single snapshot can quantify the state of data availability at time $t$, but cannot explain latency or attrition without stage-resolved events and a consistent denominator. Longitudinal evaluation could compare sample cohorts by collection month, estimate distributions of time to first laboratory result, and distinguish expected lag from logistical loss. It could also assess whether results from different analytical methods arrive at different rates and whether repeated deposits faithfully reflect the enrichment history.

Quality assessment presents a parallel challenge: detecting inconsistent metadata is not equivalent to verifying an error. Planned-country assignment and recorded collection location can disagree because both are accurate descriptions of different events. Recording the reason for a flag and its eventual resolution would permit estimates of screening precision and recoverability while avoiding the unnecessary deletion of valid field and laboratory evidence.

\subsection{Limitations}
Co-design sessions and continuing feedback informed development according to the project team, but no systematic sample of workshop records, formal requirements-coding procedure or feedback-to-commit traceability analysis is reported. Consequently, we cannot estimate stakeholder participation effects, attribute particular interface functions to specific groups, or demonstrate improved usability. The web and API features document implementation, not their causal origin or user-level benefit. This distinction also limits the strength of the design-science conclusions from a single operational deployment.

This is a single-deployment case study without an alternative architecture or a longitudinal before-and-after comparison. The coverage evaluation is cross-sectional and reflects publication filters as well as delays in laboratory processing. The target of 16,000 is a campaign objective, not a fixed population of valid, shipped and successfully analysed samples, and laboratory protocols and eligibility may differ across analytical domains. Full physical-sample tracking and laboratory stage histories were not integrated into the present evaluation. The report of 396 QA flags has not been reconciled with the canonical population or independently adjudicated. Representative sampling, ecological correlations and spatial selection bias were not evaluated, and the current coverage percentages cannot support claims about any of these properties.

The canonical six-table export does not provide a universal analytical-run identifier, which limits tracing of repeated analyses and technical replicates. Spatial jitter is not guaranteed anonymisation; border and coastline artefacts can mislead fine-scale spatial analysis. Automated image redaction may miss identifying features, and a post-mirror processing workflow does not ensure that original unredacted files were never exposed. FAIR-oriented metadata and versioned publication do not by themselves demonstrate complete semantic interoperability or discovery in downstream catalogues. Finally, the exact export code version and independent figure-reproduction scripts still need to be pinned in the final reproducibility package.

\section{Conclusions}
ECHOREPO provides an operational case study of a stakeholder-informed, sample-centred infrastructure for large-scale soil citizen science. Its value does not depend on novel database technologies: it arises from combining familiar and maintainable components with complementary interfaces, persistent research identities, incremental laboratory enrichment, contextual quality checks and independently versioned canonical publication. The published 29 October 2026 snapshot represents 10,554 physical samples and populated links among observations, photographs, laboratory parameters and biodiversity summaries. Because laboratory work is still underway within a campaign targeting approximately 16,000 samples, the observed domain-coverage values are intermediate publication states, not final analytical completion rates. Source-side reports identify 396 geographical or allocation-consistency flags, but validated error and recovery rates have not been established. The case suggests design principles for evolving environmental research-data infrastructures: allow stakeholder-facing services to change while preserving the canonical data contract, distinguish anomaly detection from provenance-based correction, and separate live operational data from reproducible releases. Documentation of individual co-design episodes, correction outcomes, temporal enrichment and independent reuse remains necessary to evaluate these principles beyond their present operational demonstration.
